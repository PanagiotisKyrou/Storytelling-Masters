# Akira Kurosawa — action, motivation, environment, tempo, editing

Use this dossier when the story needs clear changing relations, motivated action, multiple forces, environment as an active participant, previsualization, or ruthless editorial discipline. Do not reduce Kurosawa to samurai imagery, weather, or dynamic blocking.

Read evidence anchors `K1–K5` in [master-evidence-ledger.md](master-evidence-ledger.md). They ground the operations below; they are not copy for the artifact.

## Source caveat

Several direct quotations below are reproduced by the independent Akira Kurosawa Information site and attributed to *Something Like an Autobiography* or Toho's 1975 advice notes. Treat them as primary material through an intermediary, not an official estate publication.

## Research findings

### Structure can move by tempo — P-I

Kurosawa's notes compare screenplay structure to symphonic movements with differing tempos and discuss the `jo-ha-kyu` progression of introduction, destruction, and haste.

**Operational inference — S:** design state and tempo changes rather than forcing a universal three-act template. Establish, vary, disturb, accelerate/contract, and recompose as the material requires.

**Use limit:** `jo-ha-kyu` is one source model, not a mandatory three-beat shell. Preserve the source material's natural movement count and ending condition.

### Character vitality limits author control — P-I

Kurosawa warns that characters have their own existence and lose vitality when manipulated like puppets.

**Operational inference — S:** actions must follow goals, knowledge, incentives, emotion, and situation. Do not move a person or object only because the thesis needs a diagram.

### Behavior is stronger than explanation — P-I

His screenplay notes call explanatory passages dangerous and prefer psychological state expressed through nuances of action and dialogue.

**Operational inference — S:** before adding a label, ask whether timing, blocked action, decision, posture, or interaction can make the state inferable.

### Multiple writers resist one-sided interpretation — P-I

Kurosawa describes writing with others to avoid a one-sided interpretation of a person and the director's tendency to push hero and plot into the easiest pattern.

**Operational inference — S:** test the event from relevant positions before locking causality. Not every viewpoint needs to appear in the final artifact.

### Technique begins as a production solution — P-I

Kurosawa explains that multiple cameras on *Seven Samurai* began because complex rain-soaked action could not be repeated identically. He later developed distinct camera roles and movements.

**Operational inference — S:** trace a technique to the narrative/production problem it solves. Do not copy the technique after its problem disappears.

The concrete production problem matters: rain-soaked group action in *Seven Samurai* could not be repeated identically shot by shot. Multiple cameras protected irreproducible action before they became part of a broader method.

### Movement should follow the actor — P-I

His notes argue that camera movement should relate naturally to actor movement rather than announce itself.

**Operational inference — S:** coordinate camera, person, group, environment, and edit as one relational system. State what information changes because each layer moves.

### Motivation must be communicated — P-I

Kurosawa's acting notes stress that a performer must understand why an action happens, not only what to do.

**Operational inference — S:** every gesture, reaction, and transition needs an internal or situational reason.

### Editing ignores sunk cost — P-I

Kurosawa says the audience does not know how difficult a shot was and that uninteresting or unnecessary material must be cut regardless of labor.

**Operational inference — S:** production effort has no editorial vote. Judge the rendered beat by audience effect and sequence need.

### Preparation can be exhaustive — P-I/C

His notes describe rehearsing actors, camera, and lighting together; Criterion archive materials and collaborator accounts emphasize practical preparation for complex sequences.

**Operational inference — S:** previsualize high-planability relations and transitions, then let execution evidence correct the plan.

### Editing during production can expose the emerging whole — P-I

Kurosawa describes editing the day's rushes before showing them to the crew, so they could understand why difficult fragments mattered and recover energy from seeing the assembled result.

**Operational inference — S:** for long visual sequences, assemble and review partial renders during production. Do not wait until every frame is polished to discover that the sequence has no movement or that the team is solving incompatible pictures.

## State vector

Describe the opening and ending with the same variables.

| Variable | Opening | Ending |
|---|---|---|
| actors/forces | Who or what can act? | Who or what can act now? |
| goals | What is each force trying to achieve? | What changed or remained? |
| information | Who knows what? | What was learned, hidden, or misread? |
| constraints | What prevents action? | What constraint tightened, broke, or shifted? |
| environment | What conditions shape behavior? | How did the environment participate or change? |
| equilibrium | What currently holds the system together? | What new relation now holds—or fails to? |

## Motivation and movement ledger

| Element | Required answer |
|---|---|
| `action` | What exactly happens? |
| `actor_reason` | Why would this person/force do it now? |
| `trigger` | What perception, pressure, or event releases the action? |
| `environment_job` | Does the environment constrain, reveal, separate, obscure, amplify, or recompose? |
| `viewpoint_job` | What new information or relation does camera/scale/order expose? |
| `edit_consequence` | What becomes true for the audience after the cut? |
| `sunk_cost_check` | Would this beat survive if it had been easy to make? |

## STORY protocol

1. Complete the state vector for opening and ending.
2. Identify explanatory passages and prototype behavioral alternatives.
3. Check the event from several relevant positions to expose one-sided assumptions.
4. Build tempo movements appropriate to the material; do not force `jo-ha-kyu` if the source needs another shape.
5. Define disturbance and recomposition in the world, not only in the caption.
6. Cut the most attractive beat that does not change audience experience.

## FRAME STUDIO protocol

- Map movement layers: individual, group, environment, viewpoint/camera, edit.
- Complete the motivation and movement ledger for complex beats.
- Give environment a job: constrain, reveal, separate, obscure, amplify, or recompose.
- Previsualize high-risk transitions at the target aspect ratio.
- Ensure actions and gestures have motives.
- Rehearse/test complex spatial relations before full production.
- Assemble partial renders early enough to expose sequence failures while they are still cheap to correct.
- After rendering, cut frames whose difficulty or beauty exceeds their narrative value.

## Candidate questions

- What is the world-state before and after?
- What behavior makes the state visible?
- Whose position changes the interpretation?
- Which layer should move, and what changes because it moves?
- What job does the environment perform?
- What must be cut despite the effort it took?

## Failure conditions

- Movement is added as spectacle.
- Environment is atmospheric wallpaper.
- Characters act as thesis puppets.
- Multiple viewpoints create noise without changing the decision.
- Previsualization becomes inflexible after execution reveals a better path.
- A beautiful frame survives only because it was difficult to make.
- `jo-ha-kyu`, multiple cameras, weather, or movement is copied without the production/story problem that made it useful.
- Every viewpoint is shown merely because the method requested multiple perspectives.

## When to abstain or stay secondary

Stay secondary when the work's value is a quiet associative still, unresolved semantic ambiguity, or low-planability discovery with little action or state change.
