# Takehiko Inoue — character life, bodily truth, timing, revision

Use this dossier when the work needs lived human presence, physical credibility, natural timing, changed perspective, or fine-grained correction after seeing the frame. Do not reduce Inoue to manga linework, brush texture, or exaggerated sports motion.

Read evidence anchors `I1–I4` in [master-evidence-ledger.md](master-evidence-ledger.md). They ground the operations below; they are not copy for the artifact.

## Source caveat

The core protocol uses Inoue's official statement, the official film credits, and *The First Slam Dunk* press kit. Earlier versions leaned on unofficial translations for body-axis and “person over plot” claims; those claims have been removed from the core because the production record supplies stronger, directly attributable evidence.

## Research findings

### Characters remain alive beyond the plot — P

In an official 2022 statement, Inoue says the *Slam Dunk* characters continued to feel alive to him, and that aging revealed aspects and perspectives he could not see or illustrate earlier.

**Operational inference — S:** treat a character as more than a message function. Ask what they notice, want, know, and do outside the convenience of the current thesis.

### Changed creator perspective can produce a new story — P

Inoue describes the film as emerging from perspectives accumulated over decades, including pain and limits he understood differently from his twenties. He shifted attention from youthful unlimited possibility toward people living with pain, limits, and the possibility of moving through them.

**Operational inference — S:** the same source event can yield a different story when the current point of view changes. Name that perspective rather than pretending the source itself changed.

### Story can start from rough pillars — P

In the press-kit interview, Inoue says he began the film by listing what he wanted to do and placing rough pillars.

**Operational inference — S:** begin with load-bearing moments, then discover the exact path. Do not require a fully solved outline before visual exploration.

### Rough discovery still needs an early blueprint — P

Looking back on directing, Inoue says the beginning was most important and that the original blueprint mattered because he tended to lose the bird's-eye view as he became one of the players.

**Operational inference — S:** use rough pillars for discovery, then lock a provisional whole before production becomes expensive. Revisit the whole at defined review points so local retouching does not quietly break sequence balance.

### “Life” is a frame-level criterion — P

Inoue repeatedly describes his retouching goal as giving characters life. He personally corrected many cuts and used drawn instructions for expression and timing.

**Operational inference — S:** inspect rendered characters at the level of gaze, posture, weight, expression, reaction, and timing. When the feeling is wrong, draw the correction over the actual frame or rebuild it; do not defend the prompt.

### Naturalism can be more intense than exaggeration — P

Inoue says the film should not be overly exaggerated and should feel like ordinary high-school students on a basketball court.

**Operational inference — S:** test restrained, heightened, and observational versions. Choose the one that creates credibility and force, not the loudest reaction.

### Texture must support life — P

He sought a textured, paper-like image rather than a smooth/shiny CG appearance, while still using CG to coordinate ten separately moving players.

**Operational inference — S:** combine tools according to the movement/production problem while protecting the human and material qualities the work needs.

### Hybrid technique answered two different problems — P/C

The production used motion capture and CG to coordinate ten separately moving players, a 2D-like visual treatment to avoid a recognizably generic CG finish, and hand-drawn retouching for expression and timing. Inoue's official credits include writer, director, and co-animation-character designer.

**Operational inference — S:** split the problem before choosing a medium. Use one tool for spatial/motion complexity and another for the exact human or material qualities the first tool flattens. The integration criterion is the final frame, not technical purity.

### A concrete frame changed the adaptation decision — P/C

Producer Toshiyuki Matsui pursued the project through years of proposals and pilots. Inoue says a final pilot image with a convincing hand-drawn face for Sakuragi helped move him to accept. Later production still required extensive correction and rebuilding.

**Operational inference — S:** prototype the hardest qualitative claim in a concrete frame early. A technically impressive pipeline or verbal promise is not evidence that the primary character/feeling criterion works.

### Visual correction can communicate more precisely than adjectives — P

Inoue drew directly over animation frames to correct facial expression and acting timing, then judged the revised result. He says the drawing could communicate what lengthy verbal explanation could not.

**Operational inference — S:** when “more alive,” “more natural,” or “more emotional” produces random variation, mark the actual frame: gaze direction, mouth tension, hand state, shoulder/hip relation, onset of reaction, duration, or recovery.

### Familiar material can become new through viewpoint, not repetition — P

Inoue frames the film as a new tree from the same root and wanted returning audiences to encounter *Slam Dunk* again as if for the first time. His official statement describes facets of characters becoming visible only after he aged.

**Operational inference — S:** when adapting prior work, preserve the identity-bearing root and change the viewpoint, selected life experience, or audience relation. Do not merely reproduce the old sequence with newer rendering.

## Life pass

Apply this to any important person, animal, or embodied object after rendering.

| Check | Concrete question |
|---|---|
| gaze | What is the subject looking at, avoiding, or failing to notice? |
| action reason | What makes this action happen now? |
| weight/contact | Where is support, pressure, grip, drag, or release visible? |
| asymmetry | Which small imbalance prevents a generic stock pose? |
| reaction onset | Does the reaction start after the subject could perceive the trigger? |
| expression specificity | Which feature carries the state without a generic smile/shock/sadness face? |
| recovery | What remains in the body after the main action? |
| social field | Do nearby people react from their own positions rather than in synchrony? |
| environment response | Does clothing, hair, object contact, dust, shadow, or space acknowledge the action? |

## Visual correction note

For every failed “life” frame, record:

1. the observed defect in the render;
2. the exact region or timing affected;
3. a drawable correction rather than an adjective;
4. what must remain unchanged for continuity;
5. the new render evidence that would count as fixed.

## STORY protocol

1. Identify the people, objects, or moments that must feel alive.
2. Write what each person knows, wants, notices, and can physically do.
3. Place rough pillars from the most truthful moments.
4. Turn the pillars into a provisional blueprint with an audience-state path and ending condition.
5. Generate small actions or reactions that reveal character without invented melodrama.
6. Compare restrained, heightened, and observational versions.
7. State how the current creator/audience perspective changes significance.
8. Preserve a precise human or physical residue at the ending.

## FRAME STUDIO protocol

- Run the life pass on load-bearing frames.
- Avoid simultaneous generic reactions from every person.
- Prototype the hardest “feeling of life” frame early.
- Split tool roles when movement complexity and human specificity need different methods.
- Use visual correction notes for expression and timing, not vague adjectives.
- Retouch/rebuild the actual frame until the character or object feels present.
- Keep texture, tool choice, and compositing subordinate to life and action.
- Recheck the sequence blueprint after local corrections so micro-detail does not damage the whole.

## Candidate questions

- Does this character exist beyond the message?
- What tiny physical detail proves the world understands this action?
- Where is weight, contact, resistance, or release actually visible?
- Would a restrained reaction feel more truthful?
- Which perspective became visible only with time?
- What exactly should be redrawn after seeing the render?
- What should one tool solve, and what human quality must another restore?

## Failure conditions

- “Human” means generic smiling, shock, or sadness.
- Bodies, hands, gaze, weight, or timing are physically false.
- Drama is added because the source feels quiet.
- Texture is treated as life.
- A polished render survives despite lifeless characters.
- Rough pillars are mistaken for permission to skip the whole-sequence blueprint.
- One tool is expected to solve movement complexity, material texture, and micro-expression equally well.
- Unofficial translated claims are presented as core Inoue doctrine when stronger direct evidence exists.

## When to abstain or stay secondary

Stay secondary when the task is a purely abstract diagram, a dense factual table, or a non-human system where embodied micro-truth adds no value.
